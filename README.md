## Solution 1: Foundational Concepts of Generative AI
Introduction to Generative AI
Generative Artificial Intelligence, usually called Generative AI, is a type of artificial intelligence that can create new content. This content may be text, images, music, audio, video, or computer code. Examples include a chatbot writing an answer, an image tool creating a picture from a description, and an AI coding assistant suggesting a program. Generative AI does not simply copy one complete item from its training data every time. It learns patterns from examples and uses those patterns to create a new output.
What is the difference between traditional AI and Generative AI?
Traditional AI is often used to recognise, classify, or predict something. For example, an email filter may decide whether a message is spam, and a photo app may identify a dog in an image. Generative AI focuses on producing something new. For example, it may write a new email, create an image of a dog wearing a hat, or suggest a piece of computer code. The two types can overlap, but their main goals are different.
How does Generative AI work?
Generative AI systems are trained using examples. During training, a model processes data and adjusts its internal values so that it can learn useful patterns. A language model may learn which words and ideas commonly appear together. An image model may learn relationships between visual features such as shapes, colours, and objects. After training, a user gives the model an input called a prompt. The model then generates an output based on the prompt and what it learned during training. The quality of the result depends on factors such as the training data, model design, prompt, and computing resources.
Common types of Generative AI models
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) use two neural networks called a generator and a discriminator. The generator creates samples, while the discriminator tries to tell generated samples from real ones.

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) learn a compressed representation of data and use it to generate new samples.

Transformer models are widely used for text and other sequences. They use a mechanism called attention to work with relationships between different parts of the input.

Diffusion models learn to create data by gradually reversing a process that adds noise. They are widely used in image generation.
Examples and applications
Generative AI is used in education to explain topics and help create practice questions. In healthcare, it can support research, summarise documents, and assist with certain information tasks, although expert review is essential. Businesses use it to draft emails, summarise reports, and answer common customer questions.

Artists and designers can use it to explore visual ideas. Software developers may use it to explain code or suggest code snippets. Generative AI can also help create synthetic data for testing when real data is difficult to share, provided privacy and quality are carefully considered.
Advantages of Generative AI
One advantage is speed: it can produce a first draft of text, images, or code quickly. It can help people brainstorm ideas, learn unfamiliar topics, and automate repetitive writing tasks. It can also make creative tools easier for beginners to use. However, the output still needs human judgement, especially when accuracy matters.
Limitations and responsible use
Generative AI can produce incorrect statements that sound convincing. This is sometimes called a hallucination. Its results may reflect unfair bias in the data used to train it. It can also raise concerns about privacy, copyright, security, and misuse. Users should avoid entering confidential information into tools without permission, verify important facts, and follow their institution's rules about AI use. Human review is important before using generated content in academic, professional, or high-impact situations.
Conclusion
Generative AI is an important area of modern AI because it can create new content from learned patterns. Understanding its basic working principles, major model types, applications, and limitations helps people use it more effectively and responsibly.

## Solution 2: Generative AI Architectures (including Transformers)
What is a Generative AI architecture?
An architecture is the design or structure of a model. It describes the main parts of the model and how information moves through them. Different architectures are suited to different kinds of data and tasks. For example, a model that generates text must handle sequences of tokens, while a model that generates images must learn visual patterns.
1. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
A GAN contains two neural networks: a generator and a discriminator. The generator tries to create realistic samples, such as images. The discriminator tries to decide whether a sample is real or generated. During training, the generator improves by learning to fool the discriminator, while the discriminator improves at detecting generated samples. This competition can produce realistic results.

GANs have been used for image generation, image enhancement, and some image-to-image tasks. Training GANs can be difficult because the two networks must be balanced.
2. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
A VAE has an encoder and a decoder. The encoder converts an input into a compact representation called a latent representation. The decoder uses a sample from this representation to reconstruct the input or generate a new example.

The model learns a smooth latent space, which can make it possible to generate variations of the data. VAEs are used in image generation, representation learning, and research involving synthetic data. Their generated images may sometimes appear less sharp than those produced by other methods.
3. Transformers
Transformers are neural network architectures designed to process sequences and relationships between items in a sequence. They became especially important in language processing. Unlike older sequence models that process information mainly step by step, Transformers use attention to consider relationships between tokens. A token may be a word, part of a word, punctuation mark, or another small unit of text.
Important parts of a Transformer
Tokenisation divides text into tokens that a model can process. Embeddings convert tokens into numerical vectors. Positional information helps the model understand the order of tokens.

The attention mechanism allows the model to give different levels of importance to different tokens when processing an input. For example, in the sentence "The student put the book on the table because it was heavy," attention can help the model relate "it" to the relevant object based on context.
Encoder and decoder
In the original Transformer design, the encoder processes the input and creates representations, while the decoder uses these representations to produce an output sequence. Some models are encoder-only and are useful for understanding or classifying text. Some are decoder-only and generate text one token at a time.

Encoder-decoder models are useful for tasks such as translation and summarisation. Many modern chat-style language models use decoder-only Transformer designs.
4. Diffusion models
Diffusion models are commonly used to generate images. During training, noise is gradually added to training examples. The model learns how to reverse that process. During generation, it begins with random noise and repeatedly removes or predicts away noise until an image forms.

A text prompt can guide the image toward a requested subject or style. Diffusion models are also being explored for audio, video, and scientific applications.
Comparing the architectures
GANs use competition between two networks. VAEs learn a latent representation and decode it into new samples. Transformers use attention to process sequences and are widely used for language. Diffusion models learn to generate data by reversing a noise process.

These descriptions are simplified: modern systems may combine ideas from different architectures.
Conclusion
Generative AI does not rely on only one architecture. GANs, VAEs, Transformers, and diffusion models each solve generation problems in different ways. Transformers are especially important for language tasks, while diffusion models are widely used for image generation. Knowing the basic differences helps us understand how modern AI applications are built.

## Solution 4: Impact of Scaling in Large Language Models (LLMs)
Introduction
A Large Language Model (LLM) is an AI model trained to work with language, often by learning to predict the next token in a sequence. Scaling means increasing one or more parts of the training process, such as the number of model parameters, the amount of training data, or the computing resources used. Scaling has helped make language models more capable, but simply making a model larger does not guarantee that every answer will be correct.
What are parameters?
Parameters are numerical values inside a model that are adjusted during training. They help the model represent patterns in data. A model with more parameters has a greater potential capacity to learn complex patterns, but it also needs appropriate training data and enough computing resources. Parameter count alone is not a complete measure of quality.
The role of training data
Training data gives a model examples from which to learn. More data can expose a model to a wider range of language, subjects, writing styles, and tasks.

However, data quality matters. Repeating low-quality information or including biased and inaccurate material can harm the model. Data must also be collected and used with attention to privacy, copyright, and other legal requirements.
The role of computing power and training time
Training large models requires powerful hardware, such as specialised accelerators, and efficient software. More computing can allow researchers to train larger models or process more data.

However, training can be expensive and energy-intensive. Researchers must decide how to divide resources between model size, training data, and the number of training steps.
Scaling laws
Scaling laws are observed relationships between factors such as model size, training data, computing budget, and model performance. Research has shown that, under particular conditions, increasing these resources can reduce a model's prediction error in a fairly predictable way.

The best results depend on balancing the resources. A very large model trained on too little data may not use its capacity effectively, while a smaller model trained well can be useful for a specific task.
Possible improvements from scaling
Scaled models may become better at following instructions, generating fluent text, summarising information, translating, and solving some multi-step problems. They may also perform better across a wider range of topics.

Some abilities appear more clearly when models reach certain sizes or are evaluated with particular prompts and methods. However, claims about new or "emergent" abilities are debated, and observed results can depend on how the ability is measured.
Limitations remain
Even very large LLMs can produce hallucinations, misunderstand context, struggle with unfamiliar or very recent information, and reflect biases in their training data. They do not automatically have reliable knowledge of every fact.

A model's fluent writing should not be confused with guaranteed truth or human-like understanding. External tools, retrieval from trusted documents, and human checking can improve reliability for many tasks.
Costs and environmental impact
Large-scale training can require substantial money, hardware, electricity, cooling, and technical expertise. Operating a model for millions of users also requires computing resources.

These costs may make advanced systems difficult for small organisations to build independently. Researchers therefore study efficient training, smaller specialised models, model compression, and ways to use computing resources more effectively.
Responsible scaling
Developers should evaluate models for accuracy, bias, privacy, security, and misuse before deployment. They should consider whether a smaller model can perform the task well enough.

High-stakes uses require stronger safeguards and expert review. Scaling should be guided by useful performance and responsible design, not size alone.
Conclusion
Scaling has played a major role in the development of LLMs by allowing them to learn from more data and use greater model capacity. It can improve performance, but it also increases costs and does not remove problems such as hallucinations or bias.

The best model is not always the biggest one; it is the model that performs reliably for the task and can be used responsibly.

## Solution 5: Large Language Models and How They Are Built
What is an LLM?
A Large Language Model, or LLM, is an AI system trained on large amounts of text and sometimes other kinds of data to process and generate language. It can perform tasks such as answering questions, summarising documents, drafting text, translating, and assisting with code.

Many modern LLMs use Transformer architectures. A common training objective is to predict the next token based on the tokens that came before it.
Step 1: Collecting training data
The first step is gathering suitable data. Depending on the model's purpose, the data may include books, articles, websites, code, and other text sources.

Developers must consider whether the data can be used legally and ethically. Private or sensitive information needs careful handling, and data sources should be selected to support the model's intended uses.
Step 2: Cleaning and preparing data
Raw data can contain duplicates, formatting problems, spam, broken text, and harmful or irrelevant material.

Data preparation may remove duplicates, filter low-quality content, standardise formats, and apply privacy-related filtering. These steps matter because the model learns from the examples it receives. Poor data preparation can reduce quality and may preserve unwanted bias.
Step 3: Tokenisation
Computers process numbers, so text is converted into smaller units called tokens. A token may be a complete word, part of a word, punctuation, or another text unit.

For example, a sentence is split into tokens and each token is mapped to a numerical ID. Tokenisation helps the model represent and process text efficiently. The exact tokens depend on the model's tokenizer.
Step 4: Choosing the model architecture
Many LLMs use a Transformer architecture. The tokens are converted into embeddings, which are numerical representations. Attention layers help the model use context from other tokens, and neural network layers transform the information.

In a decoder-only language model, the model generally predicts one next token at a time while using the preceding context. The predicted token is added to the sequence, and the process repeats to generate a response.
Step 5: Pre-training
During pre-training, the model processes a very large number of text examples and learns to predict tokens. When its prediction differs from the training target, a training algorithm adjusts the model's parameters.

This process is repeated many times using powerful computing hardware. Pre-training helps the model learn grammar, language patterns, factual associations, and some problem-solving patterns from its data, but it does not guarantee that all learned information is accurate.
Step 6: Fine-tuning and instruction training
After pre-training, a model may be fine-tuned on more specific examples. Instruction tuning uses examples of instructions and helpful responses so that the model becomes better at following user requests.

Some systems also use human feedback or preference data to encourage responses that are more useful and safer. These stages can change how a model responds, but they cannot guarantee that it will always follow instructions correctly.
Step 7: Evaluation and safety checks
Before release, developers test the model on different tasks. They may check language quality, factual accuracy, reasoning performance, bias, privacy risks, security weaknesses, and harmful outputs.

Evaluation should include both automated tests and human review where appropriate. Developers may improve the model or add safeguards when testing reveals problems. No evaluation process can prove that a model will behave perfectly in every situation.
Step 8: Deployment
Once a model is ready for use, it can be deployed through a website, mobile application, API, or an internal business system.

When a user submits a prompt, the application sends the text to the model. The model generates tokens, which are converted back into readable text and returned to the user. The service may include extra features such as content filters, document search, logging controls, and access permissions.
A simple example
Suppose a student enters the prompt, "Explain photosynthesis in simple words."

The application tokenises the prompt and passes it to the language model. The model uses the prompt and its learned patterns to predict a sequence of tokens. Those tokens form an explanation, which is then shown to the student.

The student should still compare important scientific details with trusted learning materials because an LLM can make mistakes.
Limitations of LLMs
LLMs can produce incorrect information in confident language, reflect bias, struggle with ambiguous instructions, and have limited knowledge of events beyond their training or connected sources.

Their responses also depend on the prompt and the system in which they are used. They should not be treated as a perfect source of truth. Protecting private data and checking important outputs are essential.
Conclusion
Building an LLM involves data preparation, tokenisation, architecture design, pre-training, fine-tuning, evaluation, safety testing, and deployment. Each stage contributes to the final system.

LLMs are powerful tools for language tasks, but they work best when their limitations are understood and their outputs are checked.

## Solution 1: Foundational concepts of Generative AI
What it is. Generative AI refers to models that learn the statistical patterns of their training data and use them to produce new content such as text, images, audio, code or video. A discriminative model answers "given x, which label y?", modelling P(y|x). A generative model learns P(x) or P(x|condition), which lets it sample new x.

Main model families.
• Autoregressive models generate one token at a time, each conditioned on the previous ones (GPT-style LLMs).
• Variational autoencoders (VAEs) encode data into a latent distribution and decode samples from it.
• Generative adversarial networks (GANs) train a generator against a discriminator until fakes look real.
• Diffusion models learn to remove noise step by step, so that generation starts from noise and ends at an image.
Training versus inference. Training adjusts parameters to reduce error on a huge dataset, which is expensive and done once. Inference uses the fixed parameters to answer prompts, which is cheaper per query but happens continuously.
Worked example. Given "The capital of France is", an LLM assigns high probability to " Paris". Sampling picks it, appends it, and repeats for the next token. Nothing is looked up; the answer emerges from learned patterns, which is why fluent but wrong answers (hallucinations) are possible.
Short history. Statistical language models and RNNs (1990s to 2010s); GANs (2014); the transformer (2017); GPT and BERT (2018); large-scale GPT-3 (2020); diffusion image models and chat assistants (2021 to 2022); multimodal and reasoning models (2023 onward).

## Solution 2: Generative AI architectures, focusing on transformers

Why transformers replaced RNNs/LSTMs. RNNs process tokens sequentially, which prevents parallel training and makes long-range dependencies hard to learn (vanishing gradients). The 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need removed recurrence and relied on attention, which can be computed for all positions at once on GPUs.
Self-attention. Each token is projected into a query (Q), key (K) and value (V). A token's query is compared with every key; the resulting weights mix the values:
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) · V
The division by √d_k keeps the dot products from growing too large, which would make the softmax saturate.
Multi-head attention. Several attention operations run in parallel with different learned projections, so the model can track different relationships at once (syntax, coreference, topic).
Positional encoding. Attention alone ignores order, so position information is added (sinusoidal in the original paper; rotary embeddings in many modern models).
The block. Each layer has attention, then a position-wise feed-forward network, each wrapped in a residual connection and layer normalisation. Layers are stacked dozens of times.
Input tokens -> Embedding + Position
   +-- LayerNorm -> Masked Self-Attention --+
   |                                       (+ residual)
   +-- LayerNorm -> Feed-Forward Network ---+
   |                                       (+ residual)
   (repeat N times) -> Final Norm -> Linear -> Softmax -> next token

## Solution 4: Impact of scaling in LLMs
Three axes. Model parameters, training data (tokens) and training compute. Performance, measured by loss, tends to improve smoothly and predictably as each grows.
Scaling laws. Kaplan et al. (2020, OpenAI) showed that loss falls as a power law in parameters, data and compute. Hoffmann et al. (2022, DeepMind, "Chinchilla") showed that many large models were under-trained: for a fixed compute budget, parameters and training tokens should grow roughly in proportion (about 20 tokens per parameter as a rule of thumb). Since then, practice has often trained smaller models on far more data than that, because a smaller model is cheaper to serve.
Emergent abilities. Some skills, such as multi-step arithmetic or few-shot reasoning, appear to "switch on" at larger scale. This is a contested claim: later work argued that the sudden jumps can be an artefact of the metric used, and that with smoother metrics improvement looks gradual. A safe statement is that capabilities improve with scale, but how sharply is debated.
Cost and energy. Training frontier models costs very large sums and substantial electricity; serving them to millions of users often exceeds training cost over time. Hardware supply and data-centre energy are real constraints.
Diminishing returns. Loss follows a power law, so each further improvement needs disproportionately more resources. High-quality text data is also finite, which pushes research towards synthetic data, better filtering and multimodal data.
Inference-time scaling. Newer "reasoning" models spend more computation at answer time (longer chains of thought, multiple attempts) to improve accuracy, giving another axis besides training scale.
Cost-reduction techniques.
• Mixture-of-experts: only a few expert sub-networks are active per token, so total parameters are large but compute per token is smaller.
• Quantisation: storing weights in fewer bits (for example 8-bit or 4-bit) to cut memory and speed up inference.
• Distillation: training a small model to imitate a large one.

## Solution 5: What an LLM is and how it is built

Definition. A large language model is a neural network, almost always a decoder-only transformer, with billions of parameters, trained on very large text corpora to predict the next token. From that single objective it acquires grammar, facts, style and some reasoning ability.
Pipeline.
1.  Data collection and cleaning. Gather web pages, books, code and licensed or curated data. Remove duplicates, low-quality and harmful content, and personal information; filter by language and quality.
2.  Tokenisation. Convert text to integer tokens using byte-pair encoding (BPE), which merges frequent character pairs into sub-word units. Vocabulary sizes are typically tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand tokens.
3.  Architecture design. Choose number of layers, hidden size, attention heads, context length and positional scheme. Decoder-only transformers are the standard.
4.  Pre-training. Train on trillions of tokens with the next-token prediction (cross-entropy) objective, across thousands of GPUs or TPUs using data, tensor and pipeline parallelism, mixed precision and the AdamW optimiser with learning-rate scheduling.
5.  Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Train on curated instruction-and-response examples so the model follows instructions rather than merely continuing text.
6.  Alignment. Use human or AI preference data. RLHF trains a reward model from preference comparisons and then optimises the LLM against it; alternatives such as DPO optimise directly on preference pairs. The goal is helpful, honest and safe behaviour.
7.  Evaluation. Benchmarks (knowledge, reasoning, coding, maths), human evaluation, red-teaming for safety, and checks for bias and hallucination.
8.  Deployment and inference. Serve with optimisations such as a KV cache (reusing earlier attention keys and values), batching, quantisation and speculative decoding. Sampling settings shape output: temperature controls randomness, top-p restricts sampling to the smallest set of tokens whose probability sums to p.
9.  Add-ons. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fetches relevant documents at query time and places them in the prompt, which reduces hallucination on specific or recent facts. Tool use and agents extend this further.
